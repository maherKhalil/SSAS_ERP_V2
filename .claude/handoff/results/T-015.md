### The eighth call, and why it went with them

`PromoteAssignmentOwners` sat with the seven. It is **not a refusal** — it marks parent rows modified
when child assignments change — so **its bypass throws nothing and logs nothing.** A save through the
inner overload produced rows whose parent timestamps were never bumped: no exception, no log line,
just different data.

Of the eight it is **the only one whose omission nothing would ever surface.** A fence catching seven
noisy paths while leaving the silent one behind is partial in the least visible way.

**It stays last, after every refusal** — a rejected save must not leave the change tracker mutated by
a write that did not happen. That ordering was not specified and would have been a real bug the first
time a refusal fired.

### The architecture guard — reframed, because the criterion is not expressible

*"Every guard sits at the fence"* cannot be asserted: **reflection cannot read a method body**, so no
test can see which method calls a guard.

What **is** expressible is the only place a rule could hide — **a convenience-overload override.** A
context that declares neither `SaveChangesAsync(CancellationToken)` nor `SaveChanges()` has nowhere to
put a bypassable rule. That converts an unprovable claim about method bodies into a provable one about
method surface, and it is **strictly stronger** than checking whether today's rules happen to be in
the right place.

**It would have failed on `PlatformDbContext` yesterday and passes today** — which is the test
`DEC-L-016` now requires of every guard.
